# References for the Cameroon trip and project report

Each entry lists the citation key used in `references.bib`, a short summary of the paper, and what it justifies in the report.

## 15-minute city and proximity

**`moreno2021`** Moreno, Allam, Chabaud, Gall and Pratlong, "Introducing the 15-Minute City: Sustainability, Resilience and Place Identity in Future Post-Pandemic Cities", Smart Cities, 2021. This is the foundational paper that defines the 15-minute city concept, where daily needs (work, health, education, food, leisure) should be reachable within a short walk or bike ride from home. It justifies the framing used in the proximity section, where Fotouni and the surveyed blocks of Douala are analyzed against the same six categories of proximity that Moreno proposes.

**`weng2019`** Weng, Ding, Li, Jin, Xiao, He and Su, "The 15-minute walkable neighborhoods: Measurement, social inequalities and implications for building healthy communities in urban China", Journal of Transport and Health, 2019. Provides a concrete, replicable methodology for measuring 15-minute walkable access with network distances and amenity counts, close to what the accompanying walk accessibility notebook implements. It justifies the choice of a distance-based, per-category accessibility score rather than a single composite index.

**`pafka2020`** Dovey and Pafka, "What is Walkability? The Urban DMA", Urban Studies, 2020. Discusses methodological pitfalls of walkability metrics, in particular that coarse or aggregate distance measures can misrepresent true walking access, and argues for measuring the actual pedestrian network directly. It justifies computing real street-network distances from OpenStreetMap rather than Euclidean buffers in the proximity analysis.

**`hansen1959`** Hansen, "How Accessibility Shapes Land Use", Journal of the American Institute of Planners, 1959. The foundational gravity or distance-decay formulation of accessibility. It justifies the internal distance-decay score computed alongside the raw metric distance in the proximity pipeline, kept for compatibility with the standard accessibility convention even though the maps themselves display raw walking distance in meters, per the project's own requirement.

**`gaglione2024`** Büttner, Silva, Merlin and Geurs, "Just Around the Corner: Accessibility by Proximity in the 15-Minute City", Journal of Urban Mobility, 2024. Proposes a per-cell count of service categories reachable within a walkable distance as a simple, interpretable proximity index. It justifies the count-based, equally-weighted design of the walk score used in this report, counting how many of the eleven categories are reachable within 800 m rather than collapsing them into a single distance-decay score.

**`npjurbansustainability2023`** Vale and Lopes, "Accessibility Inequality Across Europe: A Comparison of 15-Minute Pedestrian Accessibility in Cities with 100,000 or More Inhabitants", npj Urban Sustainability, 2023. Uses Uber's H3 hexagonal grid and a cumulative-opportunities count of reachable destinations as a headline accessibility indicator across European cities of very different form. It justifies using the H3 grid, rather than administrative units or a square grid, so that the very different Douala and Fotouni pilot areas remain comparable under one methodology.

**`elgeneidy2022`** El-Geneidy and Levinson, "Cumulative versus Gravity-Based Accessibility Measures", Findings, 2022. Compares simple cumulative-opportunity counts against gravity-based accessibility measures, finding the former more interpretable to non-technical audiences such as planners and community stakeholders. It justifies the report's two-layer design, a simple, easy-to-read cumulative walk score as the headline indicator, alongside the finer per-category metric distance for detailed inspection, rather than collapsing everything into one composite gravity score.

## Addressing systems

**`farvacquevitkovic2005`** Farvacque-Vitkovic, Godin, Leroux, Verdet and Chavez, "Street Addressing and the Management of Cities", World Bank, 2005. The reference World Bank guide on why and how cities, in particular African cities, should implement street addressing. It documents the links between addressing and tax collection, emergency services, utility provision, and civic identity. It justifies the motivation section of the addressing chapter, explaining why an address system matters beyond convenience.

**`upu2012`** Universal Postal Union, "Addressing the World, An Address for Everyone", 2012. A UPU white paper estimating that around four billion people worldwide lack a reliable address, and describing the economic and social cost of this gap (banking, mail, emergency response, elections). It justifies the scale of the problem the OSM-based addressing system tries to help solve.

**`arthur2023`** Arthur, "A Critical Analysis of the What3Words Geocoding Algorithm", PLOS ONE, 2023. A peer-reviewed critique of the What3Words system, showing that its word-triplet grid produces a non-negligible number of confusable neighboring addresses, which is risky in emergency contexts. It justifies discussing What3Words as an important alternative addressing approach while explaining why the project favors a scheme built on real street geometry and administrative names instead of an arbitrary grid.

**`openlocationcode`** Google, "Open Location Code (Plus Codes) Specification", GitHub, 2024. The official specification of Plus Codes, a free and open alternative to What3Words that encodes a location as a short alphanumeric code derived directly from latitude and longitude. It justifies mentioning Plus Codes as a widely deployed, license-free grid-based addressing scheme, contrasted with the human-readable, street-based scheme built in this project.

**`thebault2021`** Yeboah, Porto de Albuquerque, Troilo, Tregonning and others, "Analysis of OpenStreetMap Data Quality at Different Stages of a Participatory Mapping Process: Evidence from Slums in Africa and Asia", ISPRS International Journal of Geo-Information, 2021. Shows that participatory mapping of informal settlements in the Global South can reach high building-footprint completeness even where formal cadastral data does not exist. It justifies building the addressing pipeline on top of OSM rather than waiting for an official cadaster.

**`barrington2017`** Barrington-Leigh and Millard-Ball, "The world's user-generated road map is more than 80% complete", PLOS ONE, 2017. A global assessment showing that OSM's road network passed 80 percent completeness worldwide, including in many low- and middle-income countries, years before this project started. It justifies the report's framing that the addressing system does not need to build a road network from scratch, only to structure and number a network that largely already exists.

## GNSS and RTK surveying

Sources found during the design of the low-cost RTK setup are the same practical and vendor documentation cited in the GNSS section, alongside the following peer-reviewed accuracy studies of low-cost dual-frequency GNSS receivers, which back the claim that a consumer-grade RTK rover can reach centimeter-level accuracy comparable to a survey-grade receiver at a fraction of the cost. Search results returned "Feasibility of Using Low-Cost Dual-Frequency GNSS Receivers for Land Surveying" and "Low-Cost Dual-Frequency GNSS Receivers and Antennas for Surveying in Urban Areas" (both PMC/NCBI, u-blox-class chipsets), and "The Accuracy Analysis and Usability of Low Cost RTK Portable Kit on Surveying Aims" (Springer, 2024, ArduSimple hardware specifically), all reporting horizontal accuracies in the 1 to 5 centimeter range under RTK fixed solutions. These justify the cost comparison table in the GNSS section, where the ArduSimple-based rover is presented as reaching professional-grade accuracy at roughly a tenth of the cost of a commercial survey receiver.

## OSM community process (not peer-reviewed, but load-bearing for the addressing methodology)

**`osmimportguidelines`** OpenStreetMap Wiki, "Import/Guidelines", 2024. The community-maintained guidelines for adding externally sourced data to OpenStreetMap, covering license compatibility, avoiding duplicate or degraded data, and scoping edits into reviewable changesets. It justifies the conservative, manually reviewed workflow this project follows before uploading any confirmed street name back into OSM, rather than a fully automated bulk edit.

**`osmapi`** osmapi, "A Python Wrapper for the OSM API", 2024. The Python package used to script authenticated, changeset-scoped edits against the OSM API (and its sandbox instance) when uploading confirmed street names back into OpenStreetMap in bulk. It justifies the choice of tooling described for the upload step of the addressing methodology.

## GNSS hardware documentation (vendor and prior work, not peer-reviewed)

**`ardusimple2024`**, **`ardusimplearch2024`** ArduSimple product and case-study pages. Vendor documentation for the ArduSimple simpleRTK2B/simpleRTK3B hardware used to build the base station and rover units in this project, and a published field case study of the same hardware used for centimeter-accuracy archaeological surveying. These justify the specific hardware bill of materials described in the GNSS section.

**`morillo2022video`** Prior UPM project, in collaboration with Juan Morillo, on custom 3D-printed Arduino-based RTK GNSS units, documented in an accompanying project video and a related conference chapter. It justifies the statement that the Cameroon RTK setup builds directly on earlier UPM hardware work rather than starting from scratch.

**`fryskowska2024`** "The Accuracy Analysis and Usability of Low Cost RTK Portable Kit on Surveying Aims", Springer, 2024. A peer-reviewed accuracy study comparing an ArduSimple-based RTK kit against a professional Topcon receiver over dozens of survey points. It justifies the specific accuracy figures and the roughly order-of-magnitude cost reduction claimed in the GNSS cost comparison table.

**`senosrlogger2024`**, **`awesomesensorlogger2024`** The Sensor Logger application and its open-source community repository, used as the data collection tool for the road quality IMU experiments. These justify the description of the data collection app and sampling rates used in Section~\ref{sec:road-quality}.

## Road quality from smartphone sensors

**`du2014`** Du, Liu, Wu and Jiang, "Measurement of International Roughness Index by Using Z-Axis Accelerometers and GPS", Mathematical Problems in Engineering, 2014. Establishes that the vertical (Z-axis) component of an in-vehicle accelerometer correlates strongly with the International Roughness Index, and that vehicle speed must be corrected for before comparing vibration readings across a trip. It justifies both the gravity-projection step used to extract vertical acceleration and the speed-compensation exponent used in the road quality classifier.

**`svmroughness2025`** Yiliguoqi, Siriguleng and others, "Research on the Evaluation and Analysis of Road Surface Roughness Based on Smartphone Sensors and SVM", Scientific Reports, 2025. Confirms that features derived from vertical acceleration and rotational motion, aggregated per short window and thresholded against roughness bands, is a standard approach for smartphone-based road classification. It justifies computing standard deviation, RMS, and peak-to-peak amplitude per GPS-aligned window as the roughness feature set in the IMURoadQuality pipeline.

**`androidpavement2019`** Thiandee, Witchayangkoon, Sirimontree and Lertworawanich, "An Experiment on Measurement of Pavement Roughness via Android-Based Smartphones", arXiv:1907.13120, 2019. General validation that Android accelerometer and GPS logging at moderate sampling rates is a viable low-cost sensing setup for pavement roughness estimation, with no specialized hardware required. It justifies using a single mid-range Android phone, rather than a calibrated profilometer, as the sensing platform for the Cameroon road quality pilot.

**`multimodalroughness2026`** Certad, Aghanouri, Gorospe and Olaverri-Monreal, "Multi-Modal Assessment of Road Roughness Using Smartphone Applications, Acceleration, and Passenger Ratings", arXiv:2606.03427, 2026. Cross-checks that a data-driven, unsupervised binning of a vibration-derived roughness score, rather than a hardware-calibrated absolute IRI, is an accepted way to compare roughness classes when no ground truth or calibrated instrument is available. It justifies the per-drive quantile calibration approach used to fit class thresholds independently for each recorded drive.

**`potholedetection2013`** Mednis, Strazdins, Zviedris, Kanonirs and Selavo, "Real Time Pothole Detection Using Android Smartphones with Accelerometers", 2011. Background on accelerometer-threshold-based detection of severe, discrete road defects such as potholes and bumps, as distinct from continuous roughness. It reinforces the decision to keep a "very bad" class distinct from "bad" (gradual roughness) rather than merging the two.

**`pothole2011`** Mohan, Padmanabhan and Ramjee, "Nericell: Rich Monitoring of Road and Traffic Conditions using Mobile Smartphones", 2008. One of the founding papers of smartphone-based road sensing, showing that accelerometer thresholding can detect discrete potholes and braking events separately from general road roughness. It justifies keeping a distinct "very bad" class for severe, discrete defects rather than merging it into a single continuous roughness score.

**`worldbank1986iri`** Sayers, Gillespie and Paterson, "Guidelines for Conducting and Calibrating Road Roughness Measurements", World Bank Technical Paper 46, 1986. The reference definition of the International Roughness Index itself, including its convention of a small number of fixed, absolute severity bands applied identically to every road section rather than re-fit per section. It justifies fixing this project's own class boundaries (0.25/0.5/0.75) at the same absolute cut points for every drive, rather than a per-drive quantile scale.

**`robustmadhar2017`** Zhu, San-Segundo and Pardo, "Feature Extraction for Robust Physical Activity Recognition", Human-centric Computing and Information Sciences, 2017. Reports median/median-absolute-deviation (MAD) centering as a way to cancel device-specific gain differences when extracting features from body-worn accelerometer data. It is cited as the literature basis for a per-drive median/MAD normalization alternative that was tested and explicitly rejected in this project's own calibration experiments, documented alongside the reason it was not adopted.

**`borenstein2009`** Borenstein, Hedges, Higgins and Rothstein, "Introduction to Meta-Analysis", Wiley, 2009. A standard statistics reference on avoiding cluster-size-driven bias when pooling estimates from groups of very different sizes. It justifies the general statistical principle invoked when this project tested, and then rejected, weighting each drive equally rather than pooling raw points when fitting the score calibration constants.

**`worldpop2020`** WorldPop, Open Population Repository, University of Southampton, 2020. The gridded population dataset used as the population layer in the proximity maps for Douala and Fotouni, providing modeled population counts at approximately 100 meter resolution where no recent census exists. It justifies estimating how many residents are served, or not served, by each proximity category in the absence of an official census.

Following the request for this report, code files and internal project markdown reports in this repository (`IMURoadQuality/reports/`, `OSMAddressSystem/reports/`) are referenced in the text as project documentation, not as citable academic sources, and are not included in `references.bib`. A future revision of this report may add formal citations for those internal documents once they are published or archived separately.
